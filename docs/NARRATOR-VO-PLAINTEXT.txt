(soft, small, a child's voice) I didn't eat lunch today. My mom said I shouldn't. (a quiet pause, then a low, weathered narrator) A few years ago, on a cold night in Ogden, a man walked up to the Giving Machines. An old man told him what ten dollars could do. A week of lunches, for a child who'd otherwise go without. (quiet, almost to himself) Somebody did that for me once. (gentle, unhurried) He tried to pay. He offered a folded twenty. There was no change for it. (a beat) So he bought two. (softer) He walked back into the night, and we never learned his name. (a turn, plainer now) Mateo's family is behind. Not by much. By enough. They've decided, the way frightened people do, that it's safer for him to be hungry than seen. So he folds his hands, and waits out the lunch hour. (a small pause) He's good at it. (even, matter-of-fact) Ten dollars buys a week. Forty, a month. Three hundred sixty dollars buys a child a year of lunches they would not otherwise eat. (quiet again) Somebody slides a tray in front of him. He doesn't see who. He never will. (a beat) That's the point. (gentle) No one called his parents. No one said his name. He just got to eat. (slow, with weight) Three children. The same mercy. (the last line lands alone) The kid you feed is the one who comes back.
